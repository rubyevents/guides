# How to Start a Ruby Meetup

Thinking of running your local Ruby user group? This guide has you covered. 

{% include toc.html variant="toc--mobile" heading="Contents" %}

## Why Bother

We love Ruby because not only is it a great programming language - it also has a wonderful community. And Meetups are the grass-roots that keep this community alive. An active meetup scene produces the people who later travel to conferences, give talks, and build open source.

A local meetup is where a junior developer gets inspired. It's where someone lands a job because they had the right conversation. It's where someone stuck on a bad project hears a talk that finally cracks the thing they've been fighting for weeks. None of that happens online.

You do not need big aspirations to start a meetup. All you need is to want one to exist.

## Starting From Zero

The first meetup is the hardest. You have no experience and no idea if anyone will show up.

It's fine, we've all been there. Do the minimum that gets some Rubyists into a room, and worry about everything else later.

### Your first venue

Secure a venue for a specific date. Without a place to meet, there can be no meetup.

The best first venue for a tech meetup is often an office. Many companies have meeting rooms and common spaces sitting empty in the evenings. A Ruby shop is the obvious first call, but any tech company with a suitable space works. They get their name mentioned to a room full of developers and you get a free room.

Universities, co-working spaces - or even bars or bakeries - are other options. All you need is enough chairs, somewhere to plug in a laptop, and ideally a wall or screen to project on.

### Create an event

Once you have a venue, pick a date and put up an event listing. It needs only the basics - the date, time, location, and a sentence or two about what the meetup is. You can add speakers and program details as they are confirmed.

### Get the word out

For the first event, promotion is simple. Just tell everyone you know! Email and text the Rubyists in your contacts. Message colleagues and former colleagues who write Ruby. Post in whatever Slack, Discord, or social channel your local community already uses.

That is enough for starters. The systematic side of getting people through the door comes later, once the meetup is a recurring thing.

### Five people is fine

The first event will probably be small. That's fine - it's a first event. Some of the best meetups started with a handful of people! 

When you're just starting out, ask your friends or colleagues to join - even if nobody else shows, at the very least you and your friends are gonna have a good time. And if one new person finds the event and walks into the room, they can join a friendly group.

Whatever happens, what matters is that the people who actually do show have a fun time and would come again.

## Your Format

Every meetup is different - there is no format to rule them all. Find something that works for the specific group of people who show up in your specific region.

That said, some formats are more common than others.

### Talks

The most common meetup format is two or three talks, followed by — or interspersed with — time for people to hang out and talk to each other. It works because talks give people something to react to. They create shared context that makes starting conversations easier. "What did you think of that talk?" is a much lower-stakes opening than "So, what do you work on?"

For talk length, shorter is almost always better. A talk that runs long eats into the time people came for. That is, seeing each other and talking.

Leave room on both sides of the program. Let people arrive, get some food, and talk before the first speaker begins. Give them time to stay and talk again after the final talk. Talks are important, but they should not consume the entire evening.

### Alternatives

A **hack night** where people bring projects to work on and help each other. This works well for communities where developers already know each other well enough to be comfortable working alongside strangers. It is harder to run cold, because newcomers don't know what to work on or who to ask for help, but it can be a great change of pace for a group that's been running a while and wants variety.

A **workshop** where one person leads a hands-on exercise. This requires more preparation and assumes attendees have laptops and - most importantly - a willingness to code with others. It's great when it lands, but harder to run than the alternatives.

A **panel** works when a subject benefits from several perspectives, or when you have knowledgeable regulars but nobody with a prepared talk. Each participant carries less of the evening than a solo speaker would, while the audience still gets something substantial to react to. The organizer needs to prepare questions and keep the conversation moving, so a panel is not quite the effortless fallback it first appears to be.

A **themed or collaborative meetup** gives the evening a tighter focus and can introduce two communities to each other. Paris.rb partnered with AI Tinkerers for a Ruby and AI meetup: Ruby developers got to see what was happening in the local AI community, while AI developers got a reason to look at Ruby. This works when the overlap is real. Combining two groups for the sake of making the event look bigger usually leaves both audiences wondering who it was for.

A **hangout** puts the focus on the community and nothing else. No talks, no workshops, no pressure - just drinks, food, and conversation. This works best once people already know each other, or as an occasional quieter evening when a speaker cancels. For a new group, a talk may still provide the reason and structure people need to attend.

## Promotion

If you have a meetup and nobody is around to hear about it, are you actually having a meetup?

Promotion isn't most developers' idea of fun. The good news is that meetup promotion is mostly unglamorous, repeatable work - not clever marketing.

### Invite people personally

The single most effective thing you can do is invite people directly. A message from a real person - "we're running a Ruby meetup on the 14th, you should come" - lands in a way no public post does. It says the event is worth showing up to, from someone who would know.

Go through LinkedIn and find Ruby developers in your city. Start with your own network - if you're thinking of starting a Ruby meetup, you almost certainly already know someone local. Reach out to them first. It's time-consuming, but it's the most reliable way to fill a first or second event.

If you know when you're next meetup is taking place you should take a minute announce it during your current one. The people are your target audience, obviously, and that way they can put the date in their calendar right away.

### Just keep posting

Public posts still matter. You just have to accept that most people will see one of them, not all. Post when you open sign-ups and post when you confirm a speaker. Post the week of the event, and again the day of. It feels repetitive to you - it's not for everyone else.

Give other parties involved in the meetup something to share. Your speakers want an audience, and host companies want people to know they support the community. Tag them, send them the event link, and ask them to share the event with their own networks. 


### Consistency

A meetup that happens on a recognizable rhythm becomes a fixture. People know roughly when it will return and can plan around it. They might even bring someone new.

Monthly meetups build that habit quickly, but they also require a steady supply of venues, speakers, and organizer energy. Meeting every other month leaves more time to find the next venue and speaker. A quarterly meetup asks less of its organizers and can still sustain a healthy community.

The right cadence is the most frequent one you can sustain without repeatedly cancelling or resenting the work. Predictability matters more than pretending every meetup needs to be monthly.

### Word of mouth

After six to twelve months of consistent events, promotion takes less effort. People mention the meetup when a colleague asks where to meet other Rubyists - the word spreads.

You cannot speed this up. It happens because the meetup is fun and the people who go want others to go too. The only way there is to run good events consistently and wait.

## Speakers & Talks

Getting speakers for a local meetup is less daunting than it seems, but it takes active effort. Sometimes volunteers appear on their own - just don't count on it.

### Ask directly

The best way to get speakers is to find someone who has been working on something interesting and send them a message. Not a general "Anyone want to speak?", but a direct: "I think what you've been building with Hotwire would make a great talk. Would you be up for it?"

Someone who has never given a talk before will say yes far more readily to a specific invitation than to an open call. They know you want to hear about their thing in particular.

Finding those people means you need to pay attention. An interesting Slack discussion, a post about a difficult production problem, or a story someone tells over coffee are things to watch out for. Keep an eye and ear open and don't hesitate ate to reach out to authors if you think their topic is a good fit for your meetup.

If a company is hosting the meetup, you can also ask whether someone there has something worth sharing. They already have a reason to participate, and a technical talk shows the community more about the company than a hiring slide does.

### Lightning talks

A ten-to-fifteen-minute talk is approachable for a first-time speaker. They don't have to fill forty minutes. They can show something they built, walk through a problem they solved, or share a technique they've been using.

Short talks also fit more speakers per event, which means more people get the experience of speaking in front of a room. Speakers who start at meetups often go on to give conference talks. Your meetup is the first rung.

A new speaker may need help narrowing the subject more than they need formal coaching. Ask them to explain one problem as they would to a more experienced colleague. Offer to look at an outline or listen to a practice run, but do not turn a ten-minute meetup talk into a conference production.

### Plan ahead

Finding speakers becomes much less stressful when you are not starting from zero after every event. Keep track of people who have offered to speak, regulars with interesting work, and previous speakers who may have something new.

Booking two or three months ahead gives everyone enough breathing room. Booking six months ahead can backfire. After all, developers change jobs, move away from Ruby, or lose interest in the original subject. A plan is useful, but the further out it reaches, the less real it becomes.

Check in four to six weeks before the event. That gives the speaker a reason to start preparing and gives you time to adjust if their situation has changed.

### When a speaker cancels

Speakers cancel. Work gets busy, children get sick, and sometimes the talk simply does not come together. Give people room to tell you early without making them feel guilty about volunteering.

A cancelled talk does not have to mean a cancelled meetup - especially if you have a backup plan. A previous speaker may be able to reuse a talk. A few regulars can form a panel or give short talks. One of the organizers may have something ready. If none of that works, let the evening become a social. The community still gets to meet, and the empty slot reminds everyone that the meetup needs speakers to continue.

## Money & Sponsors

Most meetups do not need much money. The venue is usually free, so the only real cost is food and drink - and even that is optional.

### Do you actually need sponsors?

Some meetups run with no budget at all. If you want to offer food and drink — which makes evenings more social and keeps people around longer — you will need either a small budget or a sponsor. Food matters most for evening events, when people arrive directly from work and may not have eaten. It does not have to be pizza, and it does not have to come out of the organizer's pocket.

The most common model for meetup food is a single sponsor who covers pizza and soft drinks in exchange for a short mention or presentation at the start of the event. It's a clean exchange that has worked for decades. The most natural sponsors are the same people who host your venue. You find sponsors much the same way you find speakers or venues - look around your local circle for companies that fit, and reach out.

Some companies will want to support the meetup without hosting it. Accepting that money may require an association, company, or other legal entity that can hold a bank account and issue an invoice. The details depend on where you live. Do not create an organization before you need one, but be aware that an informal group can eventually become a barrier to sponsorship.

### Working with host companies

For many meetups, the host company provides the room and food. In return, give them a few minutes to introduce themselves, thank them publicly, and let them share an appropriate hiring message. If you took photos, send them afterwards so the company can show how it supported the community.

Keep the exchange proportionate. A short introduction is reasonable. If one company provides the venue, most of the speakers, and the same hiring pitch every month, the community meetup gradually starts to feel like that company's recruiting event.

The next host often comes from the room you already have. Ask attendees whether their employers might be willing to host a future event. Once someone has seen a meetup in person, they understand what hosting involves and can make a much better case for it inside their company.

Rotating venues introduces the community to different local companies and avoids leaning too heavily on one host, but it also creates more work. A company willing to host whenever needed makes a valuable fallback. You can continue looking for variety without wondering whether the meetup will have a room at all.

## Running the Event

The logistics of a meetup are not complicated, but the ones that go wrong tend to go wrong quietly — you don't notice until you're standing in front of thirty people and the projector won't connect.

### Projectors are evil

Bring an HDMI adapter, and a USB-C to HDMI adapter for good measure. Confirm before the event that the venue's projector or TV is accessible. Know where the input selector is and know how to turn up the volume. The question is not if you'll have AV problems - it's when.

If the venue has no projection setup, a large TV that speakers can connect to directly often works fine for a small room. Once you're over thirty people, some form of projection becomes necessary — people in the back can't see a laptop screen.

### Help people find you

Even a perfect description of the location won't stop people from getting lost. Be reachable if someone can't find their way. Keep a sheet of paper and a marker handy to point people in the right direction, and post photos of the entrance so people know what to look for. This matters most when the venue is inside an office building.

A shared Slack or Discord can help here even if it never becomes a thriving online community. It gives attendees somewhere to ask which entrance is open without requiring you to publish your phone number.

### Share the evening

Setup and breakdown are small jobs until one person is doing all of them. Someone needs to meet the host, arrange the room, test the screen, welcome speakers, watch the entrance, and leave the space as it was found. None of this requires a large volunteer team, but it helps to decide who is doing what before people arrive.

The organizer fixing the projector cannot also notice that a newcomer has entered alone. Give a co-organizer or trusted regular one part of the evening to own, even if that part is simply watching the door and welcoming people.

### Name tags

When a meetup is new and attendees don't know each other, name tags genuinely help. They let people approach strangers without the awkwardness of asking for a name they missed. They also give introverts a lower-friction way to read the room and decide who to talk to.

Once your meetup has a core of regulars who all know each other, name tags matter less. But in the beginning they do real work.

### Start on time

Start within ten minutes of the announced time, even if not everyone has arrived. People who showed up on time shouldn't be penalized for the people who didn't. Starting late just trains everyone to arrive late.

It's a small thing that signals that you are organized, you respect people's time, and the meetup is a real event.

## Building Culture

A meetup's culture is not what you write in the code of conduct. Culture is built by what is tolerated, what is celebrated, and what the organizer visibly cares about.

### Make the code of conduct real

Post your code of conduct somewhere visible and mention it briefly at the start of the event. It doesn't have to be detailed. Just tell people what this community expects, and what to do if something unpleasant happens. You'll find that people notice when an organizer takes this seriously.

### Welcome new people explicitly

At the start of each meetup, ask who is here for the first time and give those people a moment of recognition. Invite regulars to introduce themselves to newcomers. Make an effort to talk to newcomers specifically. It's a small thing that dramatically changes the experience for someone arriving alone.

Asking newcomers to show themselves at the start of the meetup can be a great icebreaker. They raise their hands, and regulars are asked to notice who raised a hand and speak to them later. Raising a hand is not the welcome by itself, it just gives the rest of the room a way to act.

### Model what you expect

The standards of the room come from what you do what you permit. If you treat speakers with visible respect, your attendees will follow. If you shut down a dismissive comment in the Q&A, you signal what tone the community values. If you publicly thank the people for doing work — booking the venue, buying the food, setting up chairs — you reinforce that those contributions matter.

Consistency is key. Lead by example and show, whenever you can, what culture you'd like to build.

## Keeping It Fun

The hardest part of organizing a meetup is not the first event. It is the tenth, when the novelty has worn off, and you are tired, and you have to do it again.

### Burnout is real and predictable

Running a meetup on any frequency costs time and energy, no matter how much you enjoy it. The preparation, the back-and-forth with speakers and venues, the promotion, the evening itself — it adds up. Most organizers who burn out do so quietly. They skip one month, find a reason to skip another, and just like that the meetup stops happening.

The best prevention is to not organize the meetup alone.

### Find a co-organizer

A co-organizer is not your assistant. They are someone equally invested in the meetup continuing, who can run it when you cannot. They share the cognitive load of remembering what needs to happen and when and they cover for you when you are sick, traveling, or just depleted.

Finding a co-organizer is easier if you look for one actively rather than waiting for someone to volunteer. Ask a regular who seems particularly engaged and offer to share the role explicitly rather than asking if they "want to help out".

Give people something recurring to own. One person can find speakers while another looks for venues or handles promotion. Someone who initially offers to help a little may gradually become a genuine partner. Specific ownership gives them a way into organizing that "let me know if you can help" never does.

### Mix it up

The surest way to keep going is to keep it interesting for yourself. Running the exact same evening on repeat is how it turns into a chore. So change something! Swap talks for a hack night, move to a new venue, add a seasonal twist. Showing up with a batch of Christmas cookies costs almost nothing and livens up the whole room.

Munich Rubyshift turned the Ruby Passport into a mock Ruby Embassy. Attendees filled out deliberately silly paperwork, answered questions at the border, received a stamp, and had an instant passport photo taken. Even the volunteers playing security guards enjoyed themselves. A small ritual can give people something to participate in and make an ordinary meetup feel like your meetup.

If you're getting bored, it's likely the rest of the attendees are too. Keeping it fun for yourself is a great way to keep it fun for everyone else.

## Growing & Evolving

Most meetups that are doing well will, at some point, face the question of what comes next. These are good problems to have.

### When to grow and when not to

A meetup does not need to grow to succeed. A consistent twenty-person event in a city is worth more to the community than a hundred-person event that happens twice and stops. Chasing attendance numbers pushes you toward decisions — bigger venues, more marketing, more elaborate programming — that add overhead without adding value.

Grow when you are turning people away, when you've found a venue that supports more people at no extra cost, or when the community explicitly wants more. Don't try to grow to prove that you are succeeding.

### Connecting to the broader Ruby world

Your meetup does not exist in isolation. Ruby Central, which supports meetups and conferences globally, can connect you with other organizers, grant funding, and sponsor relationships. Joining the Ruby Central Slack and the Ruby meetup organizers' group gives you access to people who have already solved the problems you are running into.

Connecting with regional conference organizers is worth doing too. Many conferences — Rocky Mountain Ruby, Blue Ridge Ruby, Helvetic Ruby, Tropical on Rails — have explicit relationships with local meetup communities. They promote each other and share audiences. Some conferences have grown directly out of meetups. Irina Nazarova started a monthly Ruby meetup in San Francisco, which eventually became the foundation for SF Ruby Conference.

A meetup that stays connected to the broader Ruby community — through newsletters, Slack groups, conference cross-promotion, and relationships with other organizers — is less isolated when things get hard. And when things go well, that connection is how your local community becomes part of something larger.

### Handing it over

If you have been running a meetup for several years and are ready to stop, don't just let it die. Figure out a way for it to continue under someone else.

Stepping away is not a failure. After several years, your work may move away from Ruby, your interests may change, or you may simply want more space in your life. Communities change, and so do the people who sustain them.

{% include testimonial.html
  name="Dan Moore"

The most natural successor is someone who already attends, wants the meetup to survive, and understands how it works. Tell people clearly that you want to step back. Someone may care enough to take responsibility, but they cannot do that if nobody knows the meetup needs them.

The transition is easier if you have shared the organizing work rather than done everything yourself. Give someone responsibility for speaker coordination or venue booking for a few months before you hand over. By the time you step back, they will already know how the meetup works.
